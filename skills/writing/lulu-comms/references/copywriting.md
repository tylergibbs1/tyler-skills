# Copywriting

Meservey is not a direct-response copywriting teacher; her writing rules come from messaging, newsletters, speeches, media appearances, and narrative work. The standard throughout: **memorable rather than comprehensive, concrete rather than adjectival, human rather than institutional, audience-specific rather than universally agreeable.**

## The rules

- **Choose one thing the reader should remember.** Before drafting, complete: "Twenty-four hours later, I want the reader to remember ___." Remove or demote secondary points that obscure it. People retain a handful of associations; choose them deliberately. (TV guidance; Knowledge Project.)
- **Write the hook before polishing the body.** Draft five openings using curiosity, surprise, conflict, humor, or a counterintuitive fact. Pick the one most likely to matter to the defined audience. The hook outranks the story, which outranks the channel.
- **Use ordinary vocabulary.** Replace internal terminology, abstractions, and acronyms with words a smart outsider would use. Test: could a customer retell the sentence after one hearing? "Use common words. Everyone gets it. No one feels stupid." Write it so a 5-year-old could follow the idea.
- **Read it out loud.** If it's something you would never say to a friend, "you can feel the cringe surge through your body." This is the fastest corporate-speak detector.
- **Replace adjectives with proof.** Grep the draft for "innovative," "leading," "powerful," "seamless," "robust," "rapid," "best," "major." Substitute a number, customer story, demonstration, result, or observable fact. "One death is a tragedy, a million deaths is a statistic": a story beats a statistic, and a statistic beats an adjective.
- **Preserve a real person's voice.** Have the founder or subject draft, voice-note, or heavily annotate the raw material. Edit for clarity without normalizing every idiosyncratic turn of phrase. "Even if it's mid, at least it'll be mid in your voice." Honest-but-rough beats polished-but-soulless.
- **Write to a narrowly defined reader.** "Staff ML engineers at frontier labs considering a move," not "technical talent." Optimize interest for them even if outsiders don't care.
- **Make the reader benefit explicit.** Every headline or lead should answer "why should this person care?"
- **Give the reader one action.** End with a single primary next step: try, buy, apply, reply, subscribe, attend, read the evidence. Never five equally weighted CTAs.
- **Use analogy.** "Put the pill in some cheese": wrap the message in something memorable and repeatable.
- **Prefer one exceptional piece to a content treadmill.** Allocate disproportionate effort to ideas that can become reference points. Publish consistently, but never confuse frequency with value; engagement is not worth degrading quality, taste, or credibility.

## Headline diagnostic

A Meservey-compatible headline passes four tests:

1. Can the intended audience understand it immediately?
2. Is there a reason to continue reading?
3. Does it communicate something distinctive?
4. **Could a competitor publish exactly the same line without changing a word?**

If the answer to 4 is yes, the copy is category noise, not positioning.

## Voice test

Before publishing, ask:

- Would a real executive at this company say this sentence aloud?
- Could a customer understand it without industry context?
- Could a competitor publish it unchanged?
- Is there a concrete observation behind the claim?
- Does it reinforce one of the 2 to 3 things we want to be known for?

A draft failing several of these is producing corporate noise, not distinctiveness.

## Templates

Synthesized from her principles, not quotations.

**Product headline**

> **[Unexpected concrete result] for [specific audience], without [known tradeoff].**

Example: *Cut incident triage from 47 minutes to 6, without replacing your observability stack.*

**Narrative opening**

> Most **[audience]** assume **[status quo belief]**.
> That made sense when **[old condition]**.
> It no longer does because **[specific change/evidence]**.
> We built **[product]** around a different assumption: **[belief]**.

**Evidence-first launch copy**

> We started with one question: **[problem]**.
> Six months later, **[specific customers/users]** are getting **[measured outcome]**.
> Today we're releasing **[thing]** so **[audience]** can **[benefit]**.
> **[CTA].**

**Founder social post**

> **[Strong observation in one sentence.]**
>
> Here's what we saw: **[specific evidence or story].**
>
> That changed how I think about **[topic]**.
>
> So we're doing **[concrete action]**.
>
> **[Link / demo / invitation].**

## Guardrails

- Authenticity is not "publish unedited thoughts." She combines personality with message discipline, audience definition, and factual accuracy. Crisis copy prioritizes accuracy and bans speculation; TV prep bans memorized scripts but demands advance framing.
- Hook-first writing can degenerate into clickbait. Her 2026 correction: in an increasingly synthetic content environment, substantive proof becomes more valuable, not less.
