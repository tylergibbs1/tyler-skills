# Mission

Meservey treats mission not as an About-page sentence but as **an organizational belief that shapes whom you recruit, how people interpret sacrifice, what executives repeat, and what the company is willing to be disliked for.** It is simultaneously a recruiting mechanism, coordination device, story engine, and filter.

Her quality bar: clear, precise, consequential, and **unique to the company**. Generic aspirational language is worse than harmless; it signals leadership hasn't clarified what the enterprise exists to accomplish. "Define a mission that couldn't belong to any other company."

## The selection principle

Most mission advice maximizes appeal. Meservey asks whether the mission helps the right people self-select in and the wrong people self-select out. A clearly stated mission attracts believers and discourages people who will fight it after joining (Anduril: "The people who decided not to work at Anduril are people who absolutely shouldn't have worked there").

This is not license to be offensive. Her K-pop essay recommends removing irrelevant ideological barriers and reserving strong positions for matters genuinely connected to the mission. **Polarization around a core tradeoff clarifies a mission; unrelated controversy merely shrinks the coalition.**

## The rules

- **Tie mission to the actual business.** How does achieving the mission depend on the company succeeding economically? A lofty mission with no causal connection to product strategy is decorative.
- **Pass the logo-swap test.** Put a competitor's name above your mission. If it still works perfectly, rewrite until it names a distinctive objective, worldview, beneficiary, or method.
- **State a real view of the world.** What is wrong or missing today, what different future is possible, and why this company's approach can produce it.
- **Specify who benefits.** Not "make the world better." Whose condition changes, and what actually becomes better for them?
- **Include an implicit tradeoff.** What will the company prioritize when values conflict? A mission that never constrains a decision is decorative.
- **Aim high, age well.** "Aim for the moon to land among the stars"; the mission should be a guiding principle that doesn't become outdated.
- **Repeat it institutionally.** Same core mission in job postings, offer letters, interviews, onboarding, all-hands, strategy reviews, and major external narratives. Vary the stories, never the central belief. (Anduril rule: nail the narrative and repeat it "over and over again.")
- **Make leadership behavior evidence for it.** Founders and executives visibly make mission-consistent decisions, redirect recognition to mission-relevant work, and model the sacrifices expected from others.
- **Turn mission into community practices.** Rituals, milestones, stories of exemplary contributors, internal language, events, artifacts that make the abstract purpose tangible.

## Templates

A generic failure mode:

> Our mission is to transform the future of [industry] through innovative technology.

Fails the logo-swap test: any competitor could say it.

**Standard structure**

> **We exist to [specific change] for [specific people] by [distinctive mechanism or belief].**

Example: *We exist to make advanced industrial automation deployable by a five-person factory team, without requiring a systems integrator.*

**Ideological / founder-led**

> **Today, [status quo] assumes [belief]. We believe [contrary belief]. We are building [specific thing] so [specific group] can [specific future].**

**Recruiting**

> **This company is for people who believe [mission belief] strongly enough to [meaningful tradeoff]. We are trying to [concrete outcome], and the work requires [behavior/standard].**

## Mission scorecard

Rate each dimension 1 to 5 (synthesis of her principles, not a published framework):

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| Specificity | Is the intended change observable? |
| Uniqueness | Could a direct competitor say this unchanged? |
| Relevance | Does it explain why the product/company should exist? |
| Selection | Does it help aligned people opt in and misaligned people opt out? |
| Memorability | Can an employee explain it without reading it? |
| Repetition | Does it appear consistently in high-leverage company moments? |
| Proof | Do current decisions and achievements make the mission credible? |

## Guardrails

- Her most explicit mission essay is about frontier AI recruiting, an unusually ideological labor market. The transferable principle is distinctiveness and alignment, not civilization-scale rhetoric for every SaaS vendor.
- Repelling the wrong people has a real economic cost. Narrow ideological identity can unnecessarily shrink talent and customer pools; take strong positions only where intrinsic to the mission.
