# Lulu Comms

Apply how Lulu Cheng Meservey (Substack, Activision Blizzard, Anduril, Rostra) thinks about copy, mission, branding, and PR. The caricature is "traditional PR is dead." The actual system: **own your story, audience, and strategy; use journalists, agencies, and amplifiers selectively; and never run a tactic you can't trace to a business outcome.**

## The operating model

Everything else in this skill hangs off one causal chain. Work it backwards from the business objective, never forwards from a tactic:

> **Business objective → required behavior → specific audience → required belief → hook → story → human messenger → channel → evidence → repeated trust → business result.**

Her tactics formula, verbatim structure: *comms plan X will instill belief A in audience B, making them take action C, which achieves business objective D.* Define D first (limit to ~3 real objectives: recruiting, sales, fundraising, trust), then C, then B, then A, and only then generate tactics.

**The kill rule: "If you can't draw a straight solid line from a given comms tactic to a desired business outcome, that's a sure sign of wasted motion."** Impressions, press hits, follower counts, and virality are intermediate signals, never goals. If the plan's stated goal is "get coverage," rewrite the goal before writing anything.

## Core principles

### 1. Start with the ends, not comms

Before any tweet, pitch, or launch plan, write down the top business goals and reverse-engineer the messaging (her Anduril rule #1: ends/ways/means). "Anything that doesn't help the business succeed is wasted motion." A launch cheat sheet follows directly: goal is hiring → highlight team, mission, funding. Goal is sales → highlight product benefits. Goal is fundraising → highlight founders and market need. Never mix.

### 2. Go direct: own story and strategy, delegate execution

"Going direct means crafting and telling your own story, without being dependent on intermediaries." But it does not mean going alone, ignoring media, or just posting more. Agencies can pitch, do outreach, and run launches or crises; **the story and strategy are never outsourced**. "Outsourcing comms is as bad as outsourcing code." Publish a canonical owned version of every important announcement so press, employees, and customers have a primary source, and still work with journalists when their audience or credibility advances the objective. Pick the right journalist, not the biggest publication.

### 3. The founder is the messenger, in their own voice

The best spokesperson is "the person who holds the secret knowledge upon which the enterprise is built," not the most polished one. Do the core writing in the founder's voice: "even if it's mid, at least it'll be mid in your voice." Edit for clarity without normalizing idiosyncrasy; over-training removes the personality audiences actually evaluate. But avoid single-person dependency: develop multiple recognizable executives with distinct domains, and remember employees are alternate spokespeople whether you plan for it or not.

### 4. Hook, then story, then channel

In order of how much it matters: the hook (to get attention), then how you tell the story, then where you tell it. Most people invert this and start with the channel. The opening is disproportionately important; put the one takeaway in the first sentence because the segment (or the reader) may end early.

### 5. One memorable thing, in common words

People remember 2 to 3 things about you. Don't let them be random. Before drafting, complete: "twenty-four hours later, I want the reader to remember ___," and cut what obscures it. Use common words ("Everyone gets it. No one feels stupid"). Read it out loud; "you can feel the cringe" when it's something no human would say. "Corporate-speak is a barrier to understanding and an enemy of joy."

### 6. Stories and evidence over adjectives and statistics

"If you're fighting a story with a statistic, you're losing." Replace "innovative," "seamless," "industry-leading" with a number, a customer result, a demo, or a short concrete story. Use analogy to make messages portable ("put the pill in some cheese"). Honest beats polished: "if the writing is bad, it's better for it to be bad and honest."

### 7. Communicate inside-out: concentric circles and tribal elders

Spread a message from the center: yourself (for clarity) → co-founders → executives → employees → investors → power users → wider world. Find the ~10 amplifiers who each credibly reach 10 more people. Fit the message into the audience's existing passions and worldview instead of trying to change their worldview (her "cultural erogenous zones").

### 8. Mission is a filter, not a slogan

A mission must be specific enough that a competitor could not paste its logo above it (the logo-swap test). It should attract believers and repel people who would fight it after joining: "The people who decided not to work at Anduril are people who absolutely shouldn't have worked there." Repeat it in job posts, offers, all-hands, and strategy docs; vary the stories, never the belief. Reserve polarization for tradeoffs intrinsic to the mission; unrelated controversy just shrinks the coalition.

### 9. Pick a tribe; be a hard target

"If you're trying to make nobody mad, you end up making everybody mad. You're better off picking who's going to be mad at you and for what." Apologize when wrong (specifically, with amends), defend forcefully when right; being a soft target makes attacking you "a lottery ticket." On defense, spread the force; on offense, concentrate it on a specific point. And calibrate fear: "nobody gives a shit about you... if you flop, people will just move on."

### 10. Narrative alpha now comes from reality (2026 update)

Direct CEO content is commoditized; everyone has the playbook. The scarce signal is evidence of real work: products, craftsmanship, physical events and artifacts, relationships, human imperfection, and consistency over 6 to 18 months. Watch the **ship-to-yap ratio**: if you're talking more than shipping, go quiet and build until there's real progress to show. Comms cannot indefinitely compensate for a weak reality; the story is the product, so make the product worth a story.

## Where the doctrine has limits

Apply these before copying her most combative moves:

- **Independent credibility.** A company saying "our product is excellent" is not the same as a rigorous third party concluding it. Going direct buys control, not epistemic independence; use earned media where validation matters.
- **Founder suitability.** Some founders are poor writers or volatile speakers. She says herself the approach fails for "a professional CEO who is not actually interested in doing this themselves." Communication is a skill to develop, not an automatic founder talent.
- **Scale.** A large org's CEO cannot personally carry every audience, geography, and crisis. Founder-anchored, organizationally distributed.
- **Legal and regulatory reality.** Her own crisis playbook puts legal, compliance, and regulators inside the response structure. Weigh trust against legal caution deliberately; "go direct" never means ignore counsel and post. Her stances are calibrated to founder-led, mission-driven tech companies and have drawn real criticism.

## A working checklist

Run this before shipping any comms artifact:

```
Lulu review:
- [ ] Named business objective (hiring / sales / fundraising / trust)? Straight line from this artifact to it?
- [ ] Audience defined in operational terms (who decides your fate), not "the general public"?
- [ ] The one belief this must create, written down?
- [ ] Hook in the first line? Would the target audience keep reading?
- [ ] One memorable takeaway, not ten talking points?
- [ ] Adjectives replaced with numbers, stories, demos, customer proof?
- [ ] Reads aloud like a specific human? No corporate-speak, no committee prose?
- [ ] In the founder's (or a real person's) voice, not a faceless institution's?
- [ ] Hard fact addressed head-on before critics frame it?
- [ ] One primary call to action?
- [ ] Could a competitor publish this unchanged? (If yes, it's category noise.)
- [ ] Is there real shipped work behind the claim (ship-to-yap ratio)?
```
